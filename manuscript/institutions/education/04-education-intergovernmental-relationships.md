# Education Across States, Local Communities, Tribal Nations, and Territories

**The Republic We Build | ARFI**  
**Profile ID:** EDU-INST-IGV-01 | **Version:** 0.1.0 — conceptual draft  
**Level:** Intergovernmental relationships, distinct sovereigns and local learning systems  
**Reference date:** October 16, 2026 | **Status:** Open to jurisdictional and public review

> **Public purpose.** Education is experienced in families and communities. Government's responsibilities must therefore be understood through the actual law and decision-making relationships of the places where learning occurs. Equal dignity and opportunity do not require one uniform institutional system for every state, tribal nation or territory.

## 1. Institutional Identity and Present Lawful Role

### States and state education authorities

Education is primarily a **state and local responsibility**. State constitutions, statutes, regulations and administrative structures establish school governance and many rights and duties. Federal statutes create additional obligations for covered programs. For example, ESSA has state accountability and reporting requirements, and IDEA assigns specific responsibilities to state educational agencies and local agencies. [EIA-SRC-01–04](../../../research/EDUCATION_INSTITUTIONAL_AUTHORITY_REGISTER.md#eia-src-01-federalstatelocal-education-relationship)

States are not interchangeable: constitutional educational provisions, school funding, districts, accountability arrangements, teacher licensure, home education and school choice rules differ. A future national analysis must respect and document those differences rather than assume a single state baseline.

### Local school systems and educational providers

Local educational agencies, school boards and schools exercise powers and duties under applicable law. Educators, administrators and specialists are key to effective learning, while families and students remain participants and rights-holders. Public and private learning arrangements, home education, technical training and independent study have different legal frameworks.

The national future-state concept must not assume that state/local responsibility means identical delivery by one category of school. It should examine whether local choices produce credible opportunity, quality, lawful safeguards and meaningful pathways.

### Tribal nations and the Bureau of Indian Education

Tribal nations exercise inherent sovereignty subject to applicable federal law. Within a distinct federal trust and statutory relationship, the **Bureau of Indian Education (BIE)** supports BIE-operated schools and grants/contracts involving tribally controlled schools. A tribally controlled school is operated by a tribe or tribal organization and is not simply an office of the BIE or a state school district. BIE may perform state-education-agency-like functions for particular programs, but statutory definitions differ and must be checked in context. [EIA-SRC-14](../../../research/EDUCATION_INSTITUTIONAL_AUTHORITY_REGISTER.md#eia-src-14-tribal-and-bie-education)

The public objective includes high-quality learning, Native languages, cultural continuity and tribal self-determination as defined with the people and governments concerned. Neither a federal nor an ARFI plan should assume that an external agency can unilaterally define those priorities.

### U.S. territories and their own education authorities

Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, American Samoa and the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands have distinct institutions, laws and federal relationships. Their presence in certain federal education programs does not make their constitutional status or eligibility identical to the states—or to each other. [EIA-SRC-16](../../../research/EDUCATION_INSTITUTIONAL_AUTHORITY_REGISTER.md#eia-src-16-us-territories-and-program-relationships)

The required next research is **territory-specific**, including local administrative responsibility, applicable federal program rules, community needs, representation and judicial remedies. This profile does not claim to complete that analysis.

Across jurisdictions, the proposed future condition is that learners have a genuine opportunity to develop useful knowledge, think independently and advance throughout life.

## 3. Capabilities and Relationships

**Quality with local agency.** Jurisdictions should be able to define locally appropriate educational practices while providing credible evidence of learning and protecting applicable rights. Evidence can support informed comparisons without erasing historical, geographic or cultural differences.

**Effective and accessible supports.** Schools and community institutions should be able to identify barriers and connect learners with appropriate services without unlawful discrimination or unnecessary exposure of private information.

**Pathways beyond compulsory schooling.** States, colleges, unions, employers, libraries, apprenticeships, tribal education providers and other organizations may contribute to adult learning. The challenge is to make opportunities legible and genuinely accessible.

**Intergovernmental clarity.** Where federal law creates a duty, the responsible actor and rights-holder should be clear. Where voluntary cooperation is preferable, it should not be mislabeled as coercive federal command.

**Independent review and continued adjustment.** Local outcomes should be evaluated transparently, with community participation and mechanisms for correcting mistakes. Statistics and personal testimony provide different kinds of evidence and should be considered carefully.

## 4. Accountability, Rights, and Boundaries

- Protect legitimate state and local decision-making under applicable federal and state law.
- Do not presume that every educational disparity supports a particular federal remedy; investigate its causes and legal context.
- Distinguish equal protection and disability duties from a proposed broader entitlement to effective learning.
- Respect tribal governments as governments and allow affected tribal authorities to inform how responsibilities are described.
- Avoid treating territories as states for all purposes or omitting territory-specific federal eligibility and responsibilities.
- Preserve educational freedom, appropriate family choices, child safeguards, due process and meaningful remedies.
- Evaluate unintended consequences for remote, rural, underserved and historically excluded communities without mandating identical institutions everywhere.

## 5. Concepts for Future Evolution

Possible directions include locally controlled improvement supported by better research access, cooperation on technical/vocational pathways, improved specialized support across sparsely populated regions, clearer information for families, and elimination of unnecessary administrative fragmentation.

In other cases, the correct direction may be preserving effective local arrangements rather than replacing them. We should not presume every difference is a failure or every standardization is an improvement.

Any statutory restructuring of federal/state authority, reform of territorial relationships or change to tribal education duties would require a separate and substantially more demanding legal and civic process.

## 6. Implications for People and Other Institutions

A child living in a remote county, an adult changing careers, a tribal community sustaining its language, and a family residing in a territory may need different practical arrangements. The relevant national promise is a credible opportunity to learn, rather than an identical classroom, funding formula or administrative label.

Congress may legislate within constitutional limits; federal executive programs may assist and enforce within their statutes; courts may adjudicate cases under controlling law. These public contributions should be coordinated or distinguished as appropriate without assuming that one institution owns all educational outcomes.

## 7. Questions and Evidence Needed

1. Which state constitutional education clauses and judicial interpretations provide enforceable standards, and how do they differ?
2. How do district finance, transport, educator supply and rural geography affect meaningful educational access?
3. Which local learning alternatives demonstrate durable competence and accessible choice?
4. What tribal education priorities and legal authorities should be described by tribal governments themselves?
5. How should BIE-operated and tribally controlled schools be represented separately in national maps and outcomes?
6. Which federal and local protections and financial arrangements apply in each U.S. territory?
7. What data-sharing or reporting relationships are lawful and useful without creating intrusive student surveillance?
8. What participation processes help communities understand and shape education without presuming public consensus?
