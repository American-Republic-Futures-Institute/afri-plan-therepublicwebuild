# The Government We Need — Institutional Reference and Future Roles

**The Republic We Build | American Republic Futures Institute (ARFI)**  
**Part III | Reference chapter INC-06 | Version 0.1.0 | Status: Draft, not ratified**  
**Baseline:** Descriptive ARFI federal institutional catalog, snapshot 2026-10-15; State institutional pilots, 2026-10-16  
**Machine-readable counterpart:** [ARFI Government Architecture — Institutional Reference Structure](https://github.com/American-Republic-Futures-Institute/afri-architecture/blob/main/docs/INSTITUTIONAL-REFERENCE-STRUCTURE.md)

> **Purpose.** The institutions of American government are instruments through which the people exercise lawful public authority. Their value should ultimately be evaluated by whether they preserve liberty, uphold the Constitution, serve communities, and make useful national outcomes possible. This chapter helps readers understand which types of public institutions exist and how their contributions could be considered. It is not a complete directory, a scorecard of today's agencies, or a proposal to reorganize government.

## 1. Begin with the republic, not the organization chart

Part II of *The Republic We Build* describes the society Americans may wish to build: education that develops useful knowledge, accessible and effective care, a productive economy, secure private information, and other long-term national conditions. Part III asks a different question: **what lawful contribution could an institution make to those outcomes, and what must it never be permitted to do in their pursuit?**

The Constitution does not assign every desirable human outcome to a single office. Congress legislates, taxes, appropriates and oversees within constitutional limits; the executive executes enacted law and performs specifically authorized functions; and the judiciary adjudicates cases within its jurisdiction. States, tribal nations, territories and local communities are governed by distinct constitutional, sovereign and statutory relationships. Private individuals, companies, associations and families are not subordinate offices of any one of these branches.

The institutional reference view therefore complements the national outcome chapters rather than replacing them. There should be **one canonical institution record** and potentially many links to outcomes, never several contradictory copies of the same agency description.

## 2. The three constitutional branches

### A. Legislative branch — Congress and its institutions

Congress comprises the House of Representatives and Senate. It has separate chambers, committees and supporting institutions. The current architecture catalog distinguishes Congress and its chambers from selected offices such as the Government Accountability Office (GAO), Congressional Budget Office (CBO), Congressional Research Service (CRS), Library of Congress and other legislative support functions.

These distinctions matter. A committee can examine public evidence and consider legislation; it cannot unilaterally enact a law, spend funds without a lawful appropriation, operate a State school, or adjudicate cases. An independent congressional support institution can inform elected lawmakers without becoming a national operational agency.

**Possible future contribution:** better public deliberation, financial clarity, lawful oversight, traceable legislative reasoning and informed consideration of the effects of proposals on national outcomes. These are subjects for research, not a demand for any particular new statute.

### B. Executive branch — Constitutional offices, departments and other institutions

The President and Vice President are constitutional offices with specified powers and relationships. The Executive Office of the President (EOP) includes selected policy and administrative components that assist within their authority. The reference catalog currently describes seven representative EOP components, including OMB, NSC and OSTP; this is not every office that has existed or may exist.

Congress has established fifteen statutory executive departments. The list below is a **navigational snapshot**, not a proposed ranking of importance and not a claim that the Cabinet itself is the source of all departmental powers.

Some significant federal entities are not Cabinet departments. The existing catalog includes selected independent bodies, including the Federal Reserve Board, Securities and Exchange Commission, Federal Trade Commission and Federal Communications Commission. Their legal independence, internal structures and appointment rules require institution-specific review; a diagram should not imply that all these entities are directed in the same way as Cabinet departments.

**Possible future contribution:** better administrative performance, legally bounded coordination, meaningful service quality, sound data, public responsiveness and resilient capabilities. A proposed national outcome does not by itself expand executive authority or permit an executive agency to displace Congress or the States.

### C. Judicial branch — Adjudication, remedies and institutional independence

The Supreme Court, federal appellate courts, district courts, selected specialized tribunals and judicial administration institutions are recorded in the federal reference catalog. The broader U.S. judicial system includes distinct State, territorial and tribal institutions not exhaustively described by that federal catalog.

The principal judicial question is how a free republic can maintain fair, independent and accessible adjudication under controlling constitutional and statutory law. A court's relationship to a department or an individual is usually a jurisdictional, procedural or rights-based relationship—not a command-chain relationship.

The architecture intentionally distinguishes court groupings, statutory or specialized tribunals, appeals, administrative support and actual judicial power. Being displayed within a judicial-facing model does not change the constitutional classification of a specialized tribunal.

**Possible future contribution:** people should have meaningful access to lawful remedies, due process, clear legal standards and courts that remain independent of political retaliation or administrative interference. The judiciary must not be described as a substitute for ordinary democratic policymaking.

## 3. The wider federation cannot be collapsed into the federal branch diagram

### States and local communities

Fifty member States have their own constitutions, branches, legal structures and enabling statutes. The national architecture currently has independently sourced institutional pilots for Virginia and Nebraska. The remaining States must not be populated with fabricated organizations merely to complete a dashboard.

Local government and school districts derive their authorities from applicable State or other lawful arrangements. A state executive branch is not a subdivision of the President's executive branch, and national outcomes do not supply an unlimited federal direction of local institutions.

### District of Columbia and United States territories

The District of Columbia is a federal district with its own arrangements, not a fifty-first State. Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, American Samoa and the Northern Mariana Islands each require separate legal and institutional treatment. The current reference structure does not claim to map all their offices or powers.

### Tribal nations

Tribal nations must be represented as governments with distinct sovereign, treaty and statutory relationships. Tribal governments are not a uniform subclass of State administrative agencies or federal executive offices. The appropriate architecture must be developed with informed participation from affected nations.

These separate perspectives are critical for education, health, infrastructure, rights, environmental stewardship and many other national goals.

## 4. An institution should be evaluated through its contributions, not its permanence

The goal of Part III is not to defend the existence of every present office or presume an office should be abolished. The appropriate questions are what lawful public functions must be performed, whether they serve people well, and what alternatives might produce better results while retaining constitutional protections.

A future institutional profile should therefore follow a consistent sequence:

1. **Identify** the office or government accurately, using its stable architecture ID, scope and dated evidence.
2. **Establish** present constitutional or statutory responsibilities and limits from primary sources.
3. **Describe** the citizen outcomes and national capabilities it might support, without assigning ownership of those outcomes.
4. **Examine** relevant contributors and dependencies, including other branches and nonfederal jurisdictions.
5. **Protect** rights, due process, independent oversight, federalism, privacy and the ability to challenge decisions.
6. **Compare** options for retaining, improving, coordinating or legally changing functions, without making a restructuring the assumed answer.
7. **Record** evidence, competing views, uncertainties, public proposals and decisions openly.

The existing [Institutional Profile Template](../_templates/INSTITUTIONAL_PROFILE_TEMPLATE.md) gives authors the corresponding seven-section chapter form. A profile is not ready to be treated as authoritative merely because a plausible institution name has been added to a diagram.

## 5. Cross-domain capabilities are different from institutions

Some essential public capabilities matter to several outcome domains. Healthcare access depends on reliable energy and communications; workforce development matters to education, clinical services, industry and cybersecurity; privacy and secure identities matter to learners, patients, consumers and citizens.

To avoid duplicate designs, the architecture introduces eight **candidate shared capabilities**, recorded once in its separate cross-domain catalog and linked to individual national outcomes. They include data stewardship, critical-service resilience, workforce development, independent evidence, public-finance transparency, accessibility and remedies, infrastructure resilience, and identity integrity.

Readers can examine the [Shared Capability Dependencies and Public Safeguards](../../research/SHARED_CAPABILITY_DEPENDENCIES.md) for the conceptual connections and risks. A capability is not a new agency, a legal authority, or a commitment to centralize its delivery. It can be supported by combinations of individual, community, commercial and governmental activity.

This distinction allows government offices to be organized by what the public recognizes—the legislative, executive and judicial branches—while the plan still considers the economy, health, education and security on their own terms.

## 6. Four domain examples of this relationship

| Desired public outcome | Institutional relationships worth studying | Shared enabling capability | What remains undecided |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| People learn useful skills throughout life. | State/local education, lawful federal education and workforce support, private training and communities. | Workforce learning; privacy-preserving information. | Specific educational delivery and financing mechanisms. |
| People obtain necessary, effective healthcare. | Providers, States, authorized HHS programs, public health authorities and courts for legal remedies. | Clinical service continuity; data stewardship; accessible recourse. | Healthcare ownership, insurance and legal guarantee design. |
| Work and enterprise support durable prosperity. | Congress, Treasury, independent monetary institutions, market regulators, States, firms and workers. | Transparent fiscal stewardship; workforce capacity; resilient infrastructure. | Tax structure, antitrust options and industry-incentive mechanisms. |
| Digital services remain safe without sacrificing liberty. | Service operators, authorized civilian cyber functions, investigative bodies and independent courts. | Secure identity and recovery; privacy; service resilience. | New digital rights, regulation and attribution procedures. |

An entry identifies a **question for study**, not a command for any named institution. Changes to a capability map may inform the manuscript, but cannot amend law or ratify a proposal.

## 7. What should be published for public review

The future architecture should make it possible to follow a trace in both directions:

**A citizen starts with a goal:** health, housing, education, work, security or another concern. They see the envisioned future, proposed success conditions, research and candidate enablers. They can then see which existing institutions have responsibilities that may be relevant and where legal uncertainty remains.

**A citizen starts with a government office:** they see the office's existing legal remit, sources, relevant outcomes, opportunities for improvement, independence and rights constraints. They can examine the evidence, understand competing alternatives and contribute reasoned comments.

These views should come from common, versioned records, not independent manually curated diagrams that drift apart.

Public questions should include whether an institution has been described accurately, whether an implied authority has a valid source, whether overlooked groups would bear costs, whether a shared capability creates unacceptable concentration of power, and whether beneficial present functions are being preserved.

## 8. Limits of this increment and next stages

The INC-06 reference is deliberately bounded. It does not provide a full office-by-office federal bureaucracy inventory, detailed agency enabling-statute register, nationally operational KPI dataset, verified State-by-State office structures, tribal or territorial inventories, or a binding government reform program.

The next outcome integration increment should develop typed national dependencies and rights constraints using evidence and explicit uncertainty. Office-level authoring can continue in parallel when specific jurisdictions and legal authorities have been sourced and reviewed. Website publication can then display those same canonical relationships without making the website itself the source of legal or policy truth.
