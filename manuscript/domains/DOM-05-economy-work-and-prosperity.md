# Economy, Work, and Prosperity — The Future We Seek

**The Republic We Build | American Republic Futures Institute (ARFI)**  
**Domain:** DOM-05 | **Document:** ARFI-PLAN-DOM-05 | **Version:** 0.1.0  
**Status:** Conceptual draft for public and expert review; not ratified  
**Related legacy subjects:** SOC-05; ECO-01, ECO-02, ECO-03, ECO-08, ECO-09; FUT-03  
**Companion domains:** DOM-06 Public Finance; DOM-08 Industry, Science and Innovation; DOM-10 Food, Water and Environment  
**Proposed first-principle relationships:** FP-02, FP-03, FP-06, FP-08, FP-09, FP-10, FP-11, FP-12, FP-17, FP-18, FP-19, FP-20

> **The future we seek.** America should be a nation where people can create useful products, build businesses, earn a decent living through meaningful work, save and invest, improve their circumstances and participate in prosperous communities. Markets should reward innovation, reliability, quality, productive contribution and good service—rather than depend on avoidable waste, opaque coercion or exclusionary manipulation. Success should be shared through genuine opportunity without requiring identical wealth or denying legitimate private rewards.

Economic vitality is essential to the republic, but a national economy does not exist merely to maximize an aggregate number. It exists through the work of people who build, teach, invent, grow food, maintain communities, invest, repair systems, organize enterprises, care for others and purchase what they need. A healthy economy allows this activity to create enduring value while giving individuals realistic ways to participate.

The vision is not hostility toward enterprise, investors, successful business owners or government workers. Capital investment, financial returns, entrepreneurial risk, skilled management and profitable companies can serve the public well. So can competitive pressure that encourages enterprises to make better products. The concern is whether our institutions and incentives distinguish this productive value from conduct that reduces genuine choice, shifts avoidable costs to others or closes off advancement.

This chapter does not prescribe a national corporate governance model, break up named businesses, prohibit an ownership class, adopt a particular income or wealth tax, or instruct the Federal Reserve to use a specific instrument. Those are separate **policy research questions** that require legal authority, empirical evidence, affected-community input and credible comparisons.

## 1. The Future We Seek

### 1.1 Markets should compete to provide something worth having

A good economy allows people and firms to succeed by solving problems. The value of a product is not the same as how frequently it is purchased or how high its seller's quarterly return becomes. Its value can include quality, useful function, durability, service, repairability, affordability, safety and the freedom to choose alternatives.

The future should make it easier for consumers to understand what they are buying and for new businesses to compete through innovation and competence. That does not imply that all expensive products are unfairly priced or that every large company behaves improperly. Economies of scale, complex research and real scarcity can legitimately raise prices; competition law evaluates context, conduct and effects.

Better product quality does not automatically mean slowing every turnover of goods. Some sectors improve rapidly through new technology, replacement or changing tastes; others benefit from durability and repair. We should seek markets where consumers are informed and prices reflect real value rather than hidden restrictions, deception or artificially manufactured dependence.

### 1.2 Work should provide a credible route to stability and advancement

People should have realistic routes into productive work, professional growth, entrepreneurship and ownership. Compensation must be examined in relationship to skills, productivity, bargaining power, local costs, family circumstances and opportunities for advancement.

We should neither promise identical incomes nor assume that every observed difference reflects fair contributions. Both individual choices and systemic conditions can matter. A useful republic examines barriers such as inadequate training, inaccessible transportation, lack of capital, discrimination, unsafe employment, geographic isolation, child-care constraints and weak local opportunity with evidence rather than ideological shortcuts.

A sound middle class is important because it represents more than a particular income bracket. It implies the ability to meet ordinary obligations, participate in community life, withstand manageable setbacks and invest in the future. A dynamic economy should allow people to move out of poverty, build security and legitimately reach higher levels of prosperity through their contributions.

### 1.3 Enterprises should benefit when people and products flourish

Owners, workers, managers, lenders, suppliers, investors and customers play different roles. Sustainable enterprises need access to capital and a return that compensates for risk; they also need skilled people, trustworthy relationships and the ability to deliver products that remain valuable.

Corporate performance should therefore be examined in multiple dimensions: investment and profitability; consumer value and quality; worker well-being and opportunity; competitive conduct; safety and environmental effects; and institutional durability. Financial statements remain essential, but they cannot alone tell us whether growth is creating or consuming valuable capacity.

The objective is not to legislate a single ideal company culture. It is to understand whether economic rules and incentives help enterprises invest patiently, develop talent, maintain quality and compete fairly—while allowing different legitimate business models to succeed.

### 1.4 Innovation and productive national capacity should be open to future generations

America should retain and expand the ability to discover, design, manufacture, trade and adapt. This includes research institutions, entrepreneurs, skilled labor, high-value goods, digital products, efficient logistics, energy systems and advanced industrial capability. Resilience comes from capable people and diversified systems, not from a simplistic assumption that every component must be manufactured domestically.

Some technologies may justify distributed domestic capacity and trusted international sourcing; others may be better supported by competitive imports, alliances or private innovation. The goal is a highly productive, adaptable economy able to sustain households, public obligations and national security without unnecessarily isolating itself.

### 1.5 Growth should improve life without degrading what makes life valuable

Macroeconomic growth measures matter. Output, investment, employment, productivity and price stability describe important conditions. Yet a rise in GDP need not imply more affordable housing, reliable healthcare, cleaner water, longer-lasting products or greater household resilience.

The future must therefore use **complementary evidence**: national accounts alongside living standards, distribution, wealth and debt, costs of essential services, ecological conditions, product quality, entrepreneurship and social mobility. We should not dismiss GDP; we should avoid asking it to answer questions it was not designed to measure.

## 2. What Success Would Mean

### 2.1 Three candidate headline measures

These are **research candidates**, not adopted statistical definitions or targeted values. No single score should collapse productive output, household opportunity, market competition and intergenerational finance into an unreviewable rating.

| Candidate measure | Proposed operational meaning | Why it matters | Required guardrail |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **EPR-K01 — Real household economic capacity** | Estimate equivalized post-tax household resources after a transparently specified essential-cost basket, with sensitivity to family and geography. | Shows what households can actually afford and retain. | Avoid pretending a national median represents every household; disclose imputation, prices, benefits, housing and population exclusions. |
| **EPR-K02 — Opportunity for sustained upward mobility** | Follow cohorts or repeated panels for changes in earnings, assets, education-to-work transitions and durable economic security. | Tests whether people can improve circumstances rather than merely move through temporary income categories. | Correct for age, household changes and selection; do not penalize disability, care work or nonfinancial life choices. |
| **EPR-K03 — Quality-adjusted productive value** | Develop sector-specific, reproducible measures of price, service, durability, repairability, innovation and competitive choice. | Evaluates what consumers and society receive in return for productive activity. | Do not invent a universal physical quality score; monitor safety, market entry, environmental costs and measurement distortion. |

**Diagnostic measures:** credible new-firm entry and persistence; workforce wage and skill progression; price and lifetime cost of selected goods; financial resilience of viable employers; industry productivity; high-quality export capacity.

**Mandatory guardrails:** official poverty and household hardship, concentration and proven unlawful conduct, consumer safety, labor and civil rights, environmental burdens, and fiscal/debt sustainability. A rising output measure cannot justify ignoring hidden harms, and a government benefit that raises wages on paper while shifting larger costs onto households should not be counted uncritically.

### 2.2 The proposed outcome catalog

| Outcome ID | Desired public condition | Proposed evidence family |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EPR-O01 | People can achieve reliable household economic security. | EPR-K01, cost burdens and hardship. |
| EPR-O02 | Work and learning create usable routes to better circumstances. | EPR-K02 and longitudinal advancement. |
| EPR-O03 | Competitive markets reward quality and consumer usefulness. | EPR-K03, product and market quality evidence. |
| EPR-O04 | Small and growing enterprises can reasonably compete and obtain capital. | Entry barriers, finance access and durable competitive participation. |

These are a draft domain structure. The first three candidate headline measures should be refined before any numerical targets are considered; the broader outcome catalog prevents neglected public interests from disappearing merely because they are harder to count.

## 4. What We Understand Today

The Census Bureau reported **real median household income of $87,460 in 2025**, a **2.6% increase** from its revised 2024 estimate. The same report found that the household-income Gini index was not significantly different between 2024 and 2025. One growing central tendency is compatible with different experiences across the distribution; neither an observed increase nor a lack of measured inequality change settles questions about housing costs, debt, wealth or opportunity. [ECO-SRC-01](../../research/INC05_CROSS_DOMAIN_EVIDENCE_REGISTER.md)

In the Census Bureau's 2025 poverty release, **10.2%** of people were in official poverty, while the **Supplemental Poverty Measure** was **13.1%** under its different resource and cost definitions. These measures illuminate distinct aspects of material well-being, and neither should be mistaken for a complete description of opportunity or household life. [ECO-SRC-02](../../research/INC05_CROSS_DOMAIN_EVIDENCE_REGISTER.md)

The BEA measures GDP as domestic production of goods and services, not as an index of consumer life satisfaction or product durability. The FTC's description of monopolization distinguishes legal competitive success from specific prohibited conduct; large size or concentrated ownership alone does not demonstrate a violation. [ECO-SRC-03–04](../../research/INC05_CROSS_DOMAIN_EVIDENCE_REGISTER.md)

Fiscal realities also matter. GAO's June 2026 analysis reported publicly held federal debt of about **$31.3 trillion as of April 2026**, and projected a troubling fiscal path under continued policies. Debt and its interest burden are part of the constraint landscape, but an official projection is conditional, and it cannot alone determine the correct mix of taxes, spending, growth and stabilization over generations. [ECO-SRC-05](../../research/INC05_CROSS_DOMAIN_EVIDENCE_REGISTER.md)

These facts establish reasons for serious inquiry, **not** a conclusion that capitalism itself is irredeemably broken or that one ownership form, tax or regulator must dominate. The focus is an economic structure that rewards productive value and remains adaptable to competing evidence.

## 5. Conceptual Pathways and Dependencies

### 5.1 Reward productive competition rather than financial appearance alone

One route would strengthen evidence and incentives for quality, innovation, honest information, service and legitimate competition. Another would improve enforcement of existing law where market conduct is genuinely unlawful. A third might alter narrowly demonstrated structural defects if the current toolkit proves inadequate.

These options need different tests. The fact that an enterprise has substantial market share, operates many subsidiaries, uses private equity financing or earns large profits does not itself prove harm or illegality. Equally, apparent compliance with a narrow rule does not settle whether a structural market problem is worth researching. The inquiry should examine market definitions, effects, entry, consumer alternatives, innovation, scale efficiencies and legal protections.

### 5.2 Make career and ownership mobility more practical

People need credible access to knowledge, skills, employment and capital. Education and workforce programs, employer training, transparent hiring, accessible transport, child-care options, entrepreneurship and responsible financial services may each affect those possibilities.

The future should allow upward movement without promising automatic wealth, compulsory career choices or a guarantee of equal outcomes. A person working in caregiving, art, a small rural business or a trade can contribute significant value that is not captured by one wage-growth measure.

### 5.3 Develop a competitive, resilient productive base

Advanced manufacturing, semiconductors, research, robotics, energy, logistics, agriculture and technology exports matter because they support both public prosperity and security. The form of national investment should be judged by observable capability, not the number of grants announced.

Domestic productive capacity can reduce some vulnerabilities. But a nationwide collection of facilities may be inefficient if it ignores energy availability, workforce, water, transport and allied input dependencies. Diversified international sourcing and domestic investment should be compared as complementary possibilities, not treated as mutually exclusive doctrines.

### 5.4 Reexamine tax and wealth mechanisms without presuming a conclusion

The founding interviews express interest in progressive income brackets, concentration of wealth, inheritance and trust structures, taxes on assets or appreciation and incentives for firms that develop people and useful products. They do not settle the constitutional form, thresholds, rates, valuation, liquidity, effects on investment or administrative feasibility of a particular tax.

Different instruments—income taxes, capital-gains taxes, estate and gift rules, state property taxes, corporate taxation, consumption taxes, or a proposed wealth tax—operate under **different legal authorities and economic incidence**. A federal annual wealth tax raises direct-tax and constitutional questions that cannot be bypassed by treating it as an established income-tax rule.

The conceptual goal is fair and sustainable financing and productive incentives, not a presumed rate schedule or an assumption that legitimate estate planning should be abolished.

### 5.5 Distinguish monetary, fiscal and real-economy capabilities

The Federal Reserve already uses multiple tools, including open-market operations, interest on reserve balances, discount facilities and repurchase operations. [ECO-SRC-06](../../research/INC05_CROSS_DOMAIN_EVIDENCE_REGISTER.md) Monetary policy cannot be reduced to a choice between printing money and changing interest rates. Nor is it equivalent to Treasury taxation, spending or debt issuance.

Creating money or reserves to acquire public debt does not make its claims disappear without further consequences. Fiscal sustainability needs economically coherent analysis of obligations, interest costs, growth, price stability, public investment, real resources and statutory institutions. This is a necessary research constraint, not a claim that all deficits are inappropriate or that the government must balance every annual budget.

### 5.6 Put consumer, worker and enterprise experience into the public conversation

The people who participate in markets should be able to identify where current practices work well, where they encounter barriers, and what tradeoffs reforms would impose. Business leaders, employees, investors, households, small firms and affected communities will often have different information.

The research process should compare their claims against independently inspectable data while preserving disagreement. A reform is more credible when it can explain who benefits, who bears cost, who might be excluded and what evidence could change the recommendation.

## 7. Rights, Tradeoffs, and Safeguards

**Private enterprise and lawful ownership.** The vision protects room for investment, entrepreneurship, legitimate private property and rewarding economic contribution. New duties and restrictions must be considered under controlling constitutional and statutory law.

**Fair competition versus concentration.** Competition analysis must distinguish prohibited conduct from legal scale, innovation and efficiencies. An automatic breakup rule for all large firms would conflict with the stated evidence-first methodology; the absence of a violation also does not make enforcement capacity or transparency irrelevant.

**Worker and consumer rights.** Safety, fraud protections, antidiscrimination duties, labor law and financial integrity apply in legally defined contexts. A productive economy should be evaluated for its effects on people doing and purchasing the work, not shareholder return alone.

**Fiscal and constitutional feasibility.** Any proposal to tax assets, unrealized gains, trust property or wealth requires a distinct legal and economic assessment. Congress cannot enact a new tax as though unresolved constitutional classification questions were already settled. Fiscal projections must distinguish gross debt, publicly held debt, deficit flows and inflation effects.

**Equity versus uniform outcomes.** Economic opportunity is not identical to equal wealth; equally, unequal distribution is not proof of fair opportunity. Research must look for justified reward, discrimination and structural barriers without making automatic causal inferences.

**Public-private comparative evidence.** Direct public service, regulated private provision and cooperative approaches should be compared on outcomes and real cost before selection; domain outcomes do not confer the legal power to acquire or run private enterprises.

**Economic liberty, privacy and digital markets.** Consumer data, credit markets, AI labor systems and digital platforms may create efficiency and choice, but also surveillance, asymmetry and unfair exclusion. Measures must account for those risks.

**International cooperation.** Resilient domestic production and allied trade networks should strengthen each other where feasible. A strategy should not impose predictable consumer costs while claiming benefits that have not been evaluated.

| Actor | Relevant existing role | Future-state contribution for inquiry | Boundary |

## 9. Questions for Continuing Research and Public Review

1. What should count as genuine consumer value across products with very different lifetimes, safety requirements and rates of innovation?
2. Can an independent, credible measure of value or durability be built without distorting innovation or legitimate preferences?
3. Which forms of market power produce actual exclusion or consumer harm, and which scale efficiencies should be preserved?
4. Which worker and enterprise incentives demonstrably improve quality, capability and long-term viability?
5. What paths most effectively connect education and skills with wages, entrepreneurship, ownership and economic mobility?
6. What combination of income, wealth, debt, costs and access best captures prosperity across different households and regions?
7. Which wealth, inheritance, trust and taxation options are constitutionally feasible, economically effective and administratively proportionate?
8. How should federal deficit/debt sustainability be defined when public investment, monetary conditions and future growth are uncertain?
9. Where does domestic industry provide strategic resilience, and where does diversified allied sourcing work better?
10. How can public research incorporate consumer, worker, business-owner and investor experience fairly?
11. What evidence would justify expanding, reducing or redesigning a present regulatory or public-financing mechanism?
12. Which economic values should be constitutional or statutory commitments, and which belong to revisable policy decisions?
