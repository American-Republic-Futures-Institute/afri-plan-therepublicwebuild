# Health and Human Well-Being — The Future We Seek

**The Republic We Build | American Republic Futures Institute (ARFI)**  
**Domain:** DOM-03 | **Document:** ARFI-PLAN-DOM-03 | **Version:** 0.1.0  
**Status:** Conceptual draft for public and expert review; not ratified  
**Related legacy subjects:** SOC-01, CAP-12, FUT-04  
**Proposed first-principle relationships:** FP-02, FP-03, FP-04, FP-06, FP-07, FP-08, FP-09, FP-12, FP-17, FP-18, FP-19, FP-20

> **The future we seek.** People should be able to obtain timely, effective, respectful and affordable health services; maintain meaningful control over personal health decisions and information; and live in communities that make health possible. The system's success should be seen in preserved life, genuine well-being, dependable access, sound clinical outcomes and freedom from avoidable financial disruption—not just in the number of insurance cards, visits, procedures or dollars spent.

Healthcare is a fundamental concern of the republic because health affects what people are able to learn, build, contribute and enjoy. Health itself is not produced only inside a clinic. Clean water, safe housing, education, public safety, nutrition, occupational conditions, community relationships and environmental quality all influence the opportunities available to people.

This chapter defines a **proposed national future**, not an enacted constitutional entitlement, a preferred health-insurance design, a decision to nationalize medical services, or a plan requiring one model for every community. The forms of public obligation, financing and delivery remain open to comparison on measurable quality, rights, affordability, resilience and practicality.

## 1. The Future We Seek

### 1.1 A pathway to effective care, not merely nominal coverage

A person who needs care should be able to identify where to seek help, obtain an appropriate assessment, understand the available choices and continue treatment without navigating an opaque sequence of financial or administrative barriers. Being insured should not be confused with actually obtaining care, nor should a high count of medical encounters be assumed to mean the encounters produced value.

The future system should make routine preventive and primary care accessible while retaining reliable specialist, emergency, hospital, rehabilitation, mental-health, dental, maternal and long-term care where appropriate. Care pathways should be clear enough that patients can understand the purpose of a decision, ask questions, seek another opinion and obtain practical help navigating complex conditions.

Access is not satisfied by a paper promise that cannot be redeemed when a person is sick. Clinical quality is not satisfied by service volume alone. Affordability is not satisfied by lowering a visible premium while concealing costs through deductibles, exclusions, waiting, distance, or future public liabilities.

### 1.2 Health and dignity should endure across the lifespan

A strong system helps children develop safely, supports adults in managing illness and disability, and preserves dignity, independence and companionship as people age. Chronic illness, disabilities, mental-health conditions and serious diagnoses should not automatically exclude people from productive and community life.

Quality and patient autonomy matter together. People should receive understandable information about alternatives, risks and likely outcomes, with attention to language, disability, culture and practical decision-making ability. Emergency circumstances and public-health obligations may involve different lawful duties, but those duties must still have clearly defined scope and safeguards.

### 1.3 Geographic location should not dictate whether care is realistic

The United States includes isolated communities, rural counties, dense urban neighborhoods, territories and tribal communities with different infrastructure, workforce and travel constraints. A workable system can use different combinations of local care, regional referral, specialists, transportation, remote consultation and emergency transport without pretending all communities need identical facilities.

The relevant question is whether needed services can be reached safely and within clinically meaningful conditions. That requires examining real travel burden, provider availability, referral delays, clinician continuity and whether services accept the forms of coverage people actually have.

### 1.4 Public health should strengthen ordinary life without normalizing exceptional powers

Preventing avoidable injury and illness is a public benefit. Surveillance of disease trends, vaccination, food and water safeguards, environmental protection, sanitation, emergency preparedness and trustworthy public guidance may all contribute—subject to specific legal authority and proportionality.

Public-health arrangements should encourage voluntary understanding and cooperation wherever feasible and retain due process and independent review when coercive measures are legally available. A public emergency should not be treated as permanent authorization for intrusive monitoring or unchecked executive authority.

### 1.5 Health research and technology should serve people, not overwhelm them

Medical research, pharmaceuticals, diagnostics, digital records, AI-assisted decision support, assistive devices and personalized care can create real possibilities. Their benefits must be tested against clinical effectiveness, safety, cost, bias, accessibility and patient autonomy.

A high-technology health system may still fail if people cannot find a clinician, navigate benefits, afford a prescription or maintain continuity of care. Conversely, efficiency improvements and better technology can free capacity for human care. The desirable outcome is meaningful clinical and personal benefit rather than technological sophistication for its own sake.

## 2. What Success Would Mean

### 2.1 Headline measures for future evaluation

The following three **candidate primary outcome measures** are intended to complement, not replace, locally and clinically appropriate assessments. Neither their precise operational definitions nor their numeric targets has been ratified.

| Candidate headline measure | Proposed operational approach | Why people would care | Required interpretive guardrail |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **HLT-K01 — Effective care opportunity** | Among people with a defined, clinically indicated need, estimate the proportion obtaining appropriate care within an evidence-based timeframe; stratify by condition and geography. | Describes whether assistance is actually reachable and useful. | Avoid apparent improvement through denial of diagnoses, rationing, excluded populations or lower clinical standards. |
| **HLT-K02 — Avoidable health burden** | Follow suitable age-standardized, cause-specific preventable or amenable mortality and morbidity, with justified risk adjustment and uncertainty. | Addresses suffering and preventable loss of life, not activity alone. | Do not attribute national mortality changes to healthcare delivery without controlling for wider social, demographic and environmental conditions. |
| **HLT-K03 — Protection from care-related financial harm** | Estimate households whose medically necessary care creates defined unaffordable out-of-pocket burdens, debts or forgone treatment; use survey and financial sources with clear population rules. | People should not be forced into avoidable economic instability to obtain necessary care. | Evaluate provider sustainability, hidden premiums and taxes, public financing and quality; do not claim zero cost or identical entitlements. |

**Diagnostic measures** to examine alongside the headline set include time from referral to appropriate treatment, care continuity, supply of relevant clinicians, patient experience and avoidable emergency use. These may be useful, but none alone demonstrates the national goal.

**Guardrail measures** must cover harms such as potentially preventable clinical errors, denied or delayed necessary care, privacy violations, geographic exclusion and the financial sustainability of public and private providers. A reduced cost figure that is achieved by reducing needed services is not a success.

### 2.2 Outcome catalog: dimensions we should not lose

| Outcome ID | Proposed public condition | Candidate indicator or evidence family |
| --- | --- | --- |
| HLT-O01 | People can find and receive clinically appropriate care. | HLT-K01 with wait, travel and access distributions. |
| HLT-O02 | Care actually improves health and reduces avoidable harm. | HLT-K02, risk-adjusted quality and clinical outcomes. |
| HLT-O03 | Families retain financial stability when care is needed. | HLT-K03 and cost-to-household analysis. |
| HLT-O04 | Mental health, disability and long-term needs receive meaningful attention. | Access, function, continuity, rights and patient-reported effects. |
| HLT-O05 | Preventive and community conditions support healthier lives. | Disease prevention, environment, nutrition, injury and public-health outcomes. |
| HLT-O06 | Rural, tribal, territorial and underserved communities are not excluded. | Disaggregated service access and real travel or specialist burden. |
| HLT-O07 | People can understand decisions and exercise meaningful health autonomy. | Informed experience, legitimate consent and access to remedies. |
| HLT-O08 | Essential services continue and recover during major disruption. | Clinically significant service continuity and safe recovery, not merely IT uptime. |
| HLT-O09 | Private medical information is appropriately protected. | Documented inappropriate uses, incident harms, access control and correction. |

This catalog is a candidate architecture, not a statistical report. Later work must define numerators, denominators, collection windows, risk adjustments, suppression and privacy rules before treating any indicator as an operational KPI.

## 4. What We Understand Today

Recent official sources show why access, cost and health outcomes must be evaluated separately. The CDC's NCHS reported **life expectancy at birth of 79.0 years in 2024**, up from 78.4 in 2023. That is a meaningful population outcome, but it is not a comprehensive score for medical practice or a causal assessment of an insurance arrangement. [HLT-SRC-01](../../research/INC05_CROSS_DOMAIN_EVIDENCE_REGISTER.md)

CMS estimated **$5.3 trillion in U.S. health spending in 2024**, or **18.0% of GDP**. Those figures describe financial resources moving through the system; they do not independently establish quality, affordable access, clinical effectiveness, or whether the distribution of financial burden is justified. [HLT-SRC-02](../../research/INC05_CROSS_DOMAIN_EVIDENCE_REGISTER.md)

The Census Bureau reported that **7.9% of people were uninsured for the entire year in 2025** under its particular CPS ASEC measure. A person with coverage may still face a clinically important barrier, while uninsured status does not by itself describe every actual care experience. [HLT-SRC-03](../../research/INC05_CROSS_DOMAIN_EVIDENCE_REGISTER.md)

HRSA's August 2026 dashboard estimated that **about one-fifth of Americans reside in designated primary-care shortage areas**. This is an administrative shortage designation, not the average waiting time for a particular appointment or a conclusion about any one local provider. [HLT-SRC-04](../../research/INC05_CROSS_DOMAIN_EVIDENCE_REGISTER.md)

These facts are sufficiently distinct that they should not be compressed into a single conclusion about American healthcare. The next research stage needs comparable measures of medical need, service availability, actual care, household finances, quality and continuity, including differences among rural and urban settings and population groups.

## 5. Conceptual Pathways and Dependencies

### 5.1 Establish reliable access while retaining options for how services are delivered

A future national framework might articulate clearer expectations for necessary access, quality, affordability and remedies. Realizing them could involve improved private markets, regulated insurers and providers, community and nonprofit services, public financing, public options, direct public operations or different combinations across services and jurisdictions.

The foundational interviews do **not** resolve a tension between delivery-neutral improvement and the possibility of public ownership where private provision demonstrably fails. This chapter therefore records **HLT-D01 — Essential-service delivery model** as open. Any comparison should evaluate the full lifecycle: service quality, geographical reach, staffing, investment, maintenance, fiscal effect, continuity, individual choice and legal authority. Public acquisition of private assets would introduce distinct constitutional, compensation and transition requirements; it cannot be treated as automatic.

The purpose is a reliable public outcome, not victory for an ownership label.

### 5.2 Make early and continuing care workable

A system that encourages timely primary care, early support and clear follow-up may reduce unnecessary hardship. That possibility requires specific clinical and economic evidence. People with chronic conditions should not face repeated administrative friction that disrupts treatment, but coordination mechanisms must not displace informed patient choice.

Likewise, attention to mental health, rehabilitation, long-term care and caregivers should reflect meaningful functioning and dignity, rather than creating isolated categories of care disconnected from the whole person.

### 5.3 Support the workforce and the communities that rely upon it

Medical service availability depends on education, clinical training, professional scope, labor conditions, financing, local infrastructure and viable practice environments. Regional models should be assessed for actual patient experience.

Different communities may reasonably combine local generalist care, visiting specialists, telehealth, referral centers and emergency transport. The core expectation is adequate, safe and timely care, not an identical map of hospitals everywhere.

### 5.4 Connect public health with the conditions of daily life

Disease prevention, clean drinking water, nutritious food, housing, physical safety, environmental quality and trustworthy risk communication interact. A national plan should map these dependencies instead of assigning every improvement in longevity to the medical sector alone.

Public-health measures involving individual liberty or health records require lawful scope, a justified purpose, independent oversight and avenues for correction. Effective collaboration should not be confused with unrestricted public-health coercion.

### 5.5 Use incentives, transparency and enforcement proportionately

Public and private actors may respond well to incentives that reward verified quality, continuity, safety and effective service. Research should examine when payment rules create perverse incentives, whether competition increases useful choice, and when existing laws need better enforcement.

Incentives cannot replace the enforcement of legal protections; a person should not lose statutory remedies while an agency tries a voluntary program. The manuscript should evaluate demonstrated results before recommending a change in legal or financial instruments.

No numerical thresholds are set here. The appropriate thresholds for particular diagnoses, clinical services and populations require competent medical research, public participation and source-defined measurement.

## 7. Rights, Tradeoffs, and Safeguards

**Legally established protections versus proposed obligations.** Federal and state law establish particular healthcare, civil-rights, privacy and program rights. The aspirational guarantee of affordable, effective healthcare in the ARFI foundational discussion is a **proposed public commitment**; it is not presented as a universally enforceable existing federal constitutional right. Its potential legal form remains undecided.

**Federalism.** Medicaid is funded jointly by federal and state governments and administered by states under federal conditions; Medicare and other federal health functions follow their own statutes. [HLT-SRC-05–06](../../research/INC05_CROSS_DOMAIN_EVIDENCE_REGISTER.md) The structure of one federal program cannot be generalized into authority over every state health service or private provider.

**Personal autonomy and medical ethics.** Informed decisions, privacy, professional care standards and appropriate legal process must constrain efficiency targets or emergency responses. The legal scope of consent and public-health authority is context-dependent and requires separate legal review.

**Delivery and financing.** Public, private, nonprofit, cooperative and mixed models may differ in quality, choice, affordability, resilience and political accountability. Strong claims about superior ownership require measured comparison and attention to unintended consequences.

**Cost, opportunity and intergenerational balance.** Resource commitments for healthcare interact with education, housing, infrastructure, defense, household finances and future public budgets. Cost-effectiveness must consider human outcomes and distribution, not solely spending reductions.

**No metric-driven exclusion.** A national system should not improve its measured result by avoiding complex patients, underserving disabled people, excluding rural regions, suppressing complaints, or declining medically necessary treatment.

**Security and privacy.** Healthcare needs secure information exchange and operational resilience, but more data collection is not a self-justifying public benefit. Any proposal to use health records for monitoring must identify a lawful purpose, limits, retention practices, redress and credible protection from misuse.

## 8. Government and Community Relationships

| Institution or partner | Existing role at high level | Potential future contribution | Verification boundary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Congress | Federal legislation, appropriations and oversight within constitutional limits. | Examine lawful financing, protections, workforce and outcome evidence. | No direct entitlement created by this draft. |
| HHS / CMS | Federal administration and oversight of specific insurance/coverage programs. | Improve authorized quality, coverage coordination and program transparency. | Each Medicare/Medicaid/CHIP provision has separate law. |
| HHS / CDC, HRSA and related bodies | Selected public health, statistical, workforce and health-access functions under law. | Strengthen evidence, prevention and access in cooperation with others. | Program-specific source review pending. |
| States and local public-health authorities | State licensing, health functions and local service arrangements under distinct laws. | Develop locally appropriate means of access, safety and service continuity. | Varies by jurisdiction; federal conditions do not erase state authority. |
| Tribal governments and territorial health institutions | Distinct sovereignty and statutory relationships. | Define context-appropriate health priorities and partnerships. | Requires government-specific consultation and primary law. |
| Courts | Adjudicate legally cognizable health-related disputes in their jurisdiction. | Preserve enforceable rights and judicial remedies where applicable. | Not a general health program manager. |
| Providers, insurers, nonprofit organizations and employers | Varied independent, contractual and regulated roles. | Develop care capabilities and incentives aligned with patient benefit. | Neither public ownership nor private ownership alone proves performance. |
| Patients, families and communities | Rights-holders, care participants and sources of meaningful experience. | Inform the conditions of acceptable care and remedies. | Feedback needs protection and representative interpretation. |

This is a preliminary institutional map, **not** a completed authority crosswalk or a proposal to transfer powers. A later increment should assign verified legal citations and specific offices before discussing binding changes.

## 9. Questions for Continuing Research and Public Review

1. What measurable definition of effective access distinguishes a booked appointment from clinically useful care?
2. How should a future health guarantee be framed—as a constitutional right, statutory entitlement, regulated access standard, public-service objective, or combination—and by which governments?
3. Which public/private/nonprofit service-delivery combinations produce the strongest outcome under comparable need and real costs? Where is public ownership warranted, if anywhere?
4. How should the full financing burden be evaluated across premiums, taxes, wages, households, employers, provider investment and public debt?
5. How can rural, tribal and territorial communities determine realistic service models without lowering the standard of human dignity?
6. How should clinical risk adjustment prevent unfair comparisons while still exposing preventable harm?
7. Which roles should medical AI and information sharing play, and which uses would undermine privacy, informed choice or clinical relationships?
8. Which existing public and private health systems already perform effectively and deserve preservation or replication?
9. What level of emergency preparedness is appropriate without granting authorities enduring exceptional power?
10. How should patient experiences, professional evidence, civil-rights obligations and democratic participation influence future revisions?
