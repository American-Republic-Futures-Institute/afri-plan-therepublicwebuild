# Cybersecurity, Privacy, and Information Integrity — The Future We Seek

**Proposed first-principle relationships:** FP-01, FP-02, FP-03, FP-04, FP-05, FP-06, FP-07, FP-13, FP-14, FP-15, FP-16, FP-18, FP-19, FP-20

> **The future we seek.** Americans should be able to live, communicate, learn, work, trade and participate in public life through digital systems that are trustworthy, resilient, and respectful of their autonomy. Critical services should withstand and recover from hostile activity. People's identities and private information should be protected from misuse. The public should be able to understand the source and reliability of important claims without surrendering lawful expression or independent judgment to a government, company or automated system.

Modern society depends on digital systems, but security is not a license to control the people those systems serve. Cyberattacks, fraud, identity compromise and disruption can harm ordinary lives. So can excessive monitoring, arbitrary restrictions, opaque profiling and public institutions claiming to decide what citizens are allowed to believe.

A durable national future requires **both** capable defense **and** meaningful freedom. The aim is to create conditions in which people and organizations can trust digital infrastructure, recover from failure, and exercise control over their information and decisions. The paper does not authorize blanket network monitoring, a domestic military cybersecurity force, compulsory identity tracking or a government-approved truth authority.

## 1. The Future We Seek

### 1.1 Ordinary digital life should be reliably safe

People use digital services to obtain care, work, bank, learn, communicate, access public benefits and maintain social relationships. They should not have to become security experts merely to avoid routine harm. Good systems provide safe defaults, clear recovery paths, accessible reporting and protections proportionate to the harm that can occur.

Security should include people with limited technical knowledge, children, older adults, people with disabilities, small businesses, rural providers and communities with few alternatives. Security tools that are available only to wealthy institutions or digitally fluent people cannot demonstrate a fair national outcome.

A functional digital economy also needs room for innovation, free software, competitive providers, technical experimentation and legitimate security research. The intended protection should not become a reason to prohibit lawful learning or consolidate every system under one vendor.

### 1.2 Essential systems must continue when attackers succeed

No complex technology environment is free of failure. The public should benefit from systems designed not only to block attacks, but also to detect serious disruption, limit cascading harm, restore essential service and learn from incidents.

The meaningful outcome is whether people can still obtain clinically important healthcare, clean water, electricity, emergency communications, transportation, financial services and essential government functions—or regain access quickly and safely when those services are interrupted.

A reported incident count may rise when detection improves. A low number of incidents may hide underreporting. Technology uptime can conceal harmful manual delays. The future should measure **people affected, service consequences, restoration and avoidable harm**, with credible safeguards against manipulating the statistics.

### 1.3 Personal data should remain protected throughout its life

Privacy is not simply an information-security feature. An encrypted database can still be used for purposes an individual did not knowingly accept, to exclude someone unfairly, or to compile a detailed profile of lawful beliefs and associations.

Digital systems should collect and retain what is necessary for a legitimate purpose, make lawful access accountable, enable appropriate correction and limit secondary uses. Individuals should have understandable ways to know which rights and remedies apply. Governments and private entities are governed by different legal frameworks, and the scope of existing privacy protections must be described accurately.

A future-looking framework should ask how privacy can be protected even when personal records are shared across providers, public programs, devices, AI systems and national borders. Technological approaches may help, but their benefit depends on governance, threat models, usability and legal protections.

### 1.4 People should be able to form independent judgments

The information environment includes journalism, government communication, civic discussion, research, advertising, social networks and private messaging. People inevitably encounter incomplete, partisan, misleading or false claims. A free republic cannot respond by assigning the government a general power to determine which lawful views citizens may express.

The legitimate public concern is narrower and more defensible: deception about identity, secretly directed foreign influence, fraud, impersonation, undisclosed sponsorship, threats and unlawful activity may warrant investigation within applicable law. Strong media literacy, transparent public evidence and disclosure of verified origin can also help people make their own decisions.

The term **cognitive autonomy** in this manuscript means an individual's capacity to understand relevant information, consider alternatives and reach conclusions without unlawful coercion, covert manipulation or mandated ideological conformity. It is a normative design concept, not a newly enacted legal right or a justification for psychological profiling.

### 1.5 Powerful security capabilities must remain constrained

Good cybersecurity may require confidential threat intelligence, specialized expertise, criminal investigation and prompt response. Those functions operate under specific authorities and oversight arrangements. A technological ability to observe or block activity is not equivalent to legal permission to use it.

A future system should make defensive assistance genuinely useful without indiscriminate surveillance. Government entities, infrastructure owners, cybersecurity firms, civil society and individuals can cooperate where law and consent permit. Different forms of assistance will be appropriate to federal agencies, private owners, state/local governments, tribal governments, territories and national-defense missions.

The strongest design is not one that assumes permanent benevolence. It is one that remains accountable if leaders, contractors or automated systems become careless, captured or hostile.

## 2. What Success Would Mean

### 2.1 Three candidate headline measures

The following indicators require methodological research before operational use. No baseline, target or legal collection authority is claimed here.

| Candidate headline measure | Proposed operational approach | Meaning to people | Guardrail against false progress |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **CYB-K01 — Essential-service continuity under cyber stress** | Report verified, consequence-weighted interruptions and safe restoration of essential public-facing services attributable to cyber incidents, with incident severity and exposed population. | Whether medical, energy, water, financial and civic systems remain useful or recover safely. | Increased detection/reporting must not appear as worse security without context; do not suppress incidents or count superficial IT uptime while service fails. |
| **CYB-K02 — Privacy and data-use protection** | Estimate substantiated harmful or unauthorized personal-data uses, exposure and effective remedies, disaggregated by institution, source and affected population. | Whether information is handled within lawful, understandable and trustworthy boundaries. | No requirement for broader personal surveillance or forced identity disclosure solely to measure privacy. |
| **CYB-K03 — Identity and transaction integrity with redress** | Estimate verified harm from fraud, account takeover, impersonation and loss of access, plus the availability and effectiveness of correction/recovery, using careful denominators. | Whether people can retain their digital identity and correct harms without losing access to essentials. | Complaint counts are not incidence; lawfully anonymous activity is not presumed suspicious and identity verification must not create exclusion. |

**Diagnostics:** time from relevant attack detection to containment and safe service restoration; independent assessments of preventable control failures; participation in useful voluntary assistance; successful redress outcomes; proportionate provenance disclosures for verified covert influence.

**Guardrails:** unlawful collection, retention or disclosure; wrongful content restrictions; false-positive account lockouts; impaired service to people who cannot use biometric or government-ID-based methods; intrusive state or private-sector tracking; unjustified secrecy and missing judicial review.

## 4. What We Understand Today

The strongest initial sources here are official **methodologies, legal rules and institutional descriptions**, not an attempted nationwide count of cyber incidents. The NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, published in 2024, establishes six high-level functions and deliberately avoids prescribing a particular technical implementation. It can help this chapter distinguish desired resilience outcomes from tools purchased or controls checked on a form. [CYB-SRC-01](../../research/INC05_CROSS_DOMAIN_EVIDENCE_REGISTER.md)

NIST also maintains a Privacy Framework for managing risks from personal-data processing. This matters because a privacy failure may arise through an authorized-but-unfair use of data even when no external attacker steals it. [CYB-SRC-02](../../research/INC05_CROSS_DOMAIN_EVIDENCE_REGISTER.md)

The federal **Privacy Act of 1974**, 5 U.S.C. § 552a, contains requirements for specified federal agency systems of records concerning individuals, with defined rights, obligations and exceptions. It should not be described as a uniform comprehensive law covering every company and personal record. [CYB-SRC-03](../../research/INC05_CROSS_DOMAIN_EVIDENCE_REGISTER.md)

CISA describes regional Cyber Security Advisors who provide assistance to critical-infrastructure owners and state, local, tribal and territorial officials. That is a useful illustration of collaboration across institutional boundaries, not evidence of a general federal right to inspect private systems without lawful authorization. [CYB-SRC-04–05](../../research/INC05_CROSS_DOMAIN_EVIDENCE_REGISTER.md)

NIST's June 2026 ransomware community profile emphasizes management, response and recovery outcomes rather than an expectation that every malicious incident can be stopped. [CYB-SRC-06](../../research/INC05_CROSS_DOMAIN_EVIDENCE_REGISTER.md)

For foreign influence, DOJ's July 2026 FARA explanation describes certain disclosure and registration rules while noting that the Act does **not** regulate the content of speech. This distinction is essential for designing an information-integrity concept that protects public understanding without licensing unlawful viewpoint control. [CYB-SRC-07](../../research/INC05_CROSS_DOMAIN_EVIDENCE_REGISTER.md)

This research does **not** yet establish a defensible nationwide baseline for cyber harm, identity compromise or privacy misuse. Complaint datasets, incident disclosures and criminal reports have different denominators and reporting incentives. Numerical national targets remain open until the measurement method and legal data authority are reviewed.

## 5. Conceptual Pathways and Dependencies

### 5.1 Strengthen secure defaults and real recovery

Public and private providers can reduce ordinary exposure when safe practices are easier to adopt than unsafe ones. Possible approaches include accessible authentication, resilient backups, error-resistant services, supported devices, supplier assurance and trusted information sharing.

However, prescriptive technical mandates may age poorly or burden small providers; flexibility must be retained. A system should be measured by the reduction in harmful consequences and ability to recover, not the number of tools installed.

### 5.2 Treat privacy as a design requirement throughout the service

The purpose of collecting data should be explicit, lawful and proportionate. A privacy-preserving future would examine minimization, retention limits, reliable access authorization, appropriate correction, secure sharing and remedies for misuse.

Privacy should remain relevant even when artificial intelligence can infer sensitive traits from information not formally labeled sensitive. Inference is not a universal license to classify or constrain an individual's life. Policy proposals should consider both legitimate data uses and risks of bias, mission creep and opaque profiling.

### 5.3 Make cyber defense assistance accessible without dissolving institutional boundaries

CISA, state and local networks, private operators, nonprofit responders and qualified professionals can contribute different services. Voluntary defensive assistance may be effective where it respects ownership and data boundaries.

The Department of Defense has important national-defense and cyber responsibilities but is **not** a general domestic civilian policing service. Proposals for military assistance must identify their specific statutory/constitutional basis, supervision and limits, rather than inferring power from operational capability.

The role of an independent court is to address legal disputes and remedies where jurisdiction permits—not to select cybersecurity software or govern threat response.

### 5.4 Reduce manipulation by increasing provenance, literacy and public evidence

Disclosed sponsorship, reliable records, clear official communications, accessible correction and critical literacy can help people evaluate claims. Alleged covert foreign direction should be examined under actual evidence and applicable law, and published attribution should identify confidence and unresolved uncertainty.

Possible assistance from platforms and researchers should not create a government-directed blacklist of lawful opinions. Nor should private automated classifiers silently become a source of irreversible consequences without due process or relevant contractual and statutory safeguards.

### 5.5 Protect the people harmed by identity errors and security decisions

A person whose account is wrongfully blocked, information is compromised or identity is impersonated should have a workable way to restore access and seek correction. Cybersecurity that repeatedly locks out people without a remedy can undermine the same essential services it is meant to protect.

Biometrics, device verification and digital identities may be helpful in context, but they require fair alternatives and strong protections against discriminatory error, retention and abuse. The correct identity or authentication mechanism depends on the risk of the transaction and the availability of proportionate options.

### 5.6 Build institutions able to learn without permanent crisis governance

Cyber threats change quickly. Security models should evolve through technical evaluation, controlled exercises, independent research, public lessons from incidents and source-grounded regulatory improvement.

Emergency powers must remain bounded by law, transparent where possible and subject to independent review. Security urgency should not defeat the foundational separation of powers or become a shortcut around public participation.

## 7. Rights, Tradeoffs, and Safeguards

**Constitutional protection and emerging norms.** Freedom of speech and association, due process, constitutional privacy protections and related statutory rules limit government conduct. The proposed concept of cognitive autonomy is not a substitute for judicially recognized law or a claim that every digital setting has identical constitutional rules.

**Government versus private actors.** The First Amendment constrains governmental action; the lawful responsibilities of private platforms, employers and vendors arise through different legal sources and agreements. Any future proposal must distinguish these contexts rather than treating all content moderation as identical state action.

**Meaningful lawful defense.** Public institutions can defend their own networks and perform specially authorized missions. Civilian assistance to private infrastructure and domestic use of military capabilities have distinct statutory, consent and constitutional issues. They are not interchangeable.

**Privacy, consent and purpose limitation.** Digital defense should not become a general justification for surveillance of lawful beliefs, routine communications, or data about uninvolved people. The precise legality of collection and surveillance depends on statute, jurisdiction, authorization and oversight.

**Evidence and attribution.** Claims of foreign direction, coordinated deception or cybersecurity responsibility require method, provenance, uncertainty and redress. Technical indicators alone may not identify the responsible human or government. Allegations without adequate evidence must not be labeled established facts.

**Human rights and accessibility.** Protective controls need usable alternatives for people who lack smartphones, biometrics, stable addresses, institutional IDs or advanced technical skills. Security should not obstruct medical care, disability services, access to justice or education.

**Defense against institutional misuse.** The system should be assessed as though a future government or private operator might abuse access and authority. Independent oversight, limits on collection, purpose restrictions, security of the data themselves, courts and lawful remedies are essential design elements.

**Intergenerational adaptability.** Quantum, AI and future technologies will alter risks and defenses. The principles of proportionality in research, lawful authority, human freedom, auditability and ability to correct misuse should remain reviewable independent of particular technical architectures.

A separate, later authority crosswalk should assign each proposed cyber capability to a specific legal basis before a reform is presented as an implementable government action.

## 9. Questions for Continuing Research and Public Review

1. How should the nation measure actual harm from cyber events without rewarding nonreporting or collection of intrusive personal data?
2. What should count as a clinically or civically significant interruption of critical infrastructure?
3. Which security protections are most usable and effective for small organizations, rural residents, older adults and people with disabilities?
4. Which personal-data rights already exist under federal and state law, and what additional legally enforceable protections, if any, should be proposed?
5. Where do voluntary civilian cyber services work well, and what legal limits should apply to government assistance?
6. How should defense and intelligence entities be separated from ordinary domestic cybersecurity and law-enforcement functions?
7. Which types of verified foreign-influence transparency can help people assess sources without policing lawful speech?
8. What public standard for attribution and confidence could be used without risking unjust accusations or disclosing protected investigative methods?
9. How can organizations enable correction of identity errors and automated security decisions without weakening security?
10. What evidence would justify a new security statute rather than more effective enforcement, voluntary cooperation or technical improvement?
11. How can privacy-preserving measurement reveal unequal cybersecurity harms without building a new surveillance system?
12. Which emerging AI and quantum-related assumptions are robust enough for present planning, and which should remain scenario stress tests?
