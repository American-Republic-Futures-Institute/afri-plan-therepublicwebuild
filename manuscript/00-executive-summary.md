# Executive Summary — The Republic We Build

**American Republic Futures Institute (ARFI)**  
**Document ID:** ARFI-PLAN-FM-01 | **Version:** 0.1.0 | **Status:** Editorial draft; open for public and expert review  
**Planning horizon:** Long-term and intergenerational, with initial review checkpoints extending through 2101 and beyond

> **The central proposition:** The United States should be judged by whether its institutions protect liberty, enable people to live securely and meaningfully, support broadly accessible prosperity, preserve democratic self-government, and sustain the capacity to defend those conditions. The purpose of reform is not to enlarge government for its own sake or dismantle it for ideological reasons. It is to make legitimate public authority more capable, constrained, accountable, and useful to the people.

## ES-01 — Why long-term improvement requires a connected view

Americans experience the republic through connected systems: the Constitution, elections and representation, courts and civil remedies, federal and state institutions, markets and employment, housing and healthcare, education, infrastructure, national defense, and public services. Each has its own law, history, actors, resources and incentives. Their outcomes, however, are interdependent. A change to taxation affects investment; investment affects employment and industrial capacity; industrial capacity affects infrastructure, national security, and the tax base. Changes to healthcare access can affect family stability, productivity, and local economic life. An attempt to improve security can simultaneously strengthen national resilience and threaten privacy if its authority is poorly designed.

Lasting improvement therefore requires more than a collection of isolated policy proposals. Changes must be considered in the context of the constitutional, economic, technical, and human systems they affect. Each idea should be examined for its legal basis, responsible institutions, practical financing, consequences for people, and ways to recognize when an approach needs adjustment.

The American Republic Futures Institute (ARFI) proposes **The Republic We Build** as an open, evolving public research initiative for that work. It takes a long-term, intergenerational view. Milestones can help organize research and evaluate progress, but neither the initiative nor its foundational commitments end at a particular date.

## ES-02 — The objective: a free, capable, prosperous, self-correcting republic

The republic we seek protects individual liberty, makes rights meaningful and enforceable, rewards economic contribution, supports dependable essential services, and maintains public institutions that can learn, respond, and correct mistakes. The proper responsibilities and methods of government should be examined openly, rather than settled by a predetermined model of how large or small government must be.

A capable republic should be able to deter aggression, assist allies, defend critical infrastructure, and protect citizens from unlawful coercion and cyber threats. A free republic must also prevent security powers from becoming instruments of indiscriminate surveillance, political censorship, or unreviewable control. A prosperous republic should reward investment, enterprise, skill and work while ensuring that markets compete through quality, affordability and productive value, rather than through unlawful manipulation or captured institutions. A democratic republic must give people meaningful opportunities to participate, disagree, and shape decisions. Public institutions should listen to those they serve, act on well-founded concerns, explain their reasoning in accessible terms, and remain willing to revise decisions through continued dialogue and evidence.

These objectives are complementary in some circumstances and in tension in others. The task of this research is to make those tensions understandable and examine lawful, evidence-grounded choices with the people affected, rather than declare the tradeoffs solved in advance.

## ES-03 — Twenty proposed first principles

The initiative begins with twenty *proposed* first principles, organized here into five connected commitments. Their authoritative wording and identifiers appear in the companion introduction and the draft foundational charter.

### The individual and the community (FP-01–FP-04, FP-07–FP-09)

Legitimate government derives from the people; human dignity and liberty constrain state power; equality before the law must be combined with meaningful opportunity; the law must provide remedy against coercive misuse; protections for persons must be distinguished from citizenship-specific political rights; essential conditions for human capability are public concerns; and community must be supported without compelled ideological conformity.

### Constitutional institutions (FP-05–FP-06, FP-18–FP-20)

Federalism and separated powers prevent dangerous concentration; government effectiveness is measured in outcomes rather than administrative activity; evidence, dissent, and uncertainty must be recorded honestly; and institutional reform must preserve the republic's ability to correct its mistakes under changing conditions.

### Markets and advancement (FP-10–FP-12, FP-17)

Productive competition, worker and consumer interests, entrepreneurship, discovery, environmental responsibility, and intergenerational fiscal credibility belong in the same framework. Neither private ownership nor public ownership automatically demonstrates good service; performance and lawful authority matter.

### Security and the world (FP-13–FP-16)

Defense must remain formidable and constitutional; data security, privacy, and cognitive autonomy deserve attention; national sovereignty can coexist with constructive international partnership; and strategic resilience requires domestic capacity, distributed infrastructure, and trusted supply relationships where the evidence supports them.

### The unifying commitment

Every instrument of public power is subordinate to legal legitimacy, human dignity, verifiable public purpose, and continued democratic accountability.

## ES-04 — An open inquiry grounded in respect and evidence

This initiative begins with respect for the people who serve the public and for the institutions, communities, and organizations that contribute to American life. Much existing work merits recognition and preservation. Our purpose is to understand how these systems function, learn from experience, identify opportunities for improvement, and support their continued ability to serve people well.

The questions examined here are often complex, with more than one reasonable interpretation or way forward. We will approach them with intellectual humility: seeking reliable evidence, recognizing established expertise, listening to affected communities, and making room for perspectives that challenge our own assumptions. The presence of disagreement is a reason for careful examination and public dialogue, not a reason to dismiss a concern or presume a particular outcome.

The Republic We Build is guided by enduring commitments to liberty, human dignity, equal standing before the law, meaningful opportunity, accountable government, effective public service, and national resilience. Those commitments establish a shared direction for inquiry; they do not predetermine the particular policies, institutions, or mechanisms that future research and public deliberation may support.

## ES-05 — How the work is organized

Government decisions affect connected parts of everyday life. To make the research understandable, we will examine each question through five practical layers: what matters, who is responsible, what should improve, how the work is done, and how a change could be put into practice. Together, these layers help us understand the full system without losing sight of the people it serves.

### L0 — Values and protections

What fundamental rights, shared commitments, and safeguards should guide our decisions? Which protections already exist in law, and which additional commitments deserve consideration?

### L1 — Responsibility and accountability

Who has the authority to act, what responsibilities do different institutions hold, and how can the public question or challenge their decisions?

### L2 — Outcomes for people

What conditions should improve in people's lives? How can we measure those changes fairly and recognize when some communities are being left behind?

### L3 — How the pieces work together

Which public services, people, processes, information, tools, resources, and relationships are needed? We will use established approaches, including the Federal Enterprise Architecture Framework (FEAF), to map these connections and make complex government operations easier to understand.

### L4 — Turning ideas into practical change

What lawful actions, resources, transition steps, community involvement, and independent evaluations would be needed to test an idea and, where appropriate, put it into practice?

Each layer informs the next. A promising idea still requires lawful authority, an understanding of its effects on people, practical means of delivery, and a way for the public to review and help improve it.

## ES-06 — How reforms will be examined

ARFI proposes twelve documented review gates, G-00 through G-11: problem and provenance; lawful authority; rights and dignity; public value and causality; distribution and equity; security and abuse resistance; federalism and dependencies; fiscal and operational feasibility; alternatives and proportionality; public participation and expert challenge; transition protection; and verification and correction.

These gates distinguish threshold legal constraints from policy optimization. A favorable benefit estimate does not authorize the violation of a binding right. A reform without adequate evidence remains a hypothesis. A reform that requires constitutional amendment must say so. Alternatives include better enforcement of existing law, revised incentives, non-governmental solutions, direct public provision where lawful, or no immediate action. Every substantial recommendation must specify who benefits, who pays, who faces harm, and how the result can be evaluated and corrected.

The public should be able to inspect reasoning, offer corrections and alternative ideas, and understand how substantial contributions are considered. Accessible consultation and transparent editorial review should be supported by appropriate safeguards against spam, impersonation, and automated manipulation. The institute itself must disclose uncertainty, donor and reviewer conflicts, methodology limitations, and dissenting interpretations.

## ES-07 — The coverage of the plan

The planned manuscript is organized into ten major parts: constitutional foundations; democratic institutions; government operations; human well-being and community; markets and public finance; industrial capacity and environment; defense and democratic security; foreign relations; science and future resilience; and implementation architecture. Appendices will maintain legal authorities, research sources, capability models, outcome definitions, fiscal assumptions, public decisions, and superseded alternatives.

This outline is a starting coverage map rather than a claim to have enumerated every government function. A national capability inventory will systematically test the manuscript's omissions and interfaces across legislative, executive, judicial, state, local, territorial and tribal institutions.

## ES-08 — Long-term milestones and measures of success

Illustrative checkpoints include **2026, 2031, 2041, 2051, 2076 and 2101**. They are opportunities to review progress, not deadlines that define the initiative's duration or numerical forecasts. Early work will establish trustworthy legal, institutional, and evidence baselines; later work can test, evaluate, and revisit reforms that demonstrate value. Looking further ahead, scenarios for 2200, 2300, and other possible futures can help test whether the foundational principles remain useful under conditions we cannot yet predict.

Success cannot be reduced to aggregate GDP, agency budgets, legislative enactments, military expenditure, or the number of modeled capabilities. It must be established through changes in people's lives and the republic's ability to sustain those changes: security without repression, better access to dependable essentials, meaningful education, productive opportunity, accountable representation, competitive value, environmental and fiscal resilience, and reliable alliances. Measurements must report who benefits and who does not.

## ES-09 — Public invitation and institutional limits

This paper is a starting point for public inquiry, not a finished national consensus. Its arguments, architecture, sources and revisions should remain open to constructive challenge, including from those who disagree with its premises. The institute can publish research and recommendations; only duly authorized governmental bodies can implement laws or exercise public powers. Proposed ARFI governance, charitable status, editorial safeguards and public review processes themselves require lawful institutional adoption.

The standard for progress is therefore not how quickly the plan gathers adherents. It is whether the evidence improves, the constitutional and institutional architecture becomes clearer, competing interests are more honestly represented, and plausible reforms emerge that demonstrably benefit the public without sacrificing the protections that give government its legitimacy.

**The Republic We Build begins with this proposition: protect the people's rights, define the outcomes they should be able to achieve, constrain power, test reforms honestly, and preserve the nation's ability to correct itself.**
